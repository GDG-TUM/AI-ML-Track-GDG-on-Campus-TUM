# 🔭 The Vision: What Is Possible

> Machine learning is the closest thing we have to a general-purpose tool for turning examples into ability. Whoever learns to use it well, and honestly, gets to decide what it is used for.

This page is not about the next six weeks. It is about **why they are worth it**: what people are already doing with this field, where it is heading, and where you could fit in.

---

## 🌍 It is already happening

None of the examples below is science fiction. Each one exists today, and each one is built from the same pieces you learn in [Phase 1](../roadmap/semester-roadmap.md).

| Where | What machine learning made possible | The idea underneath | Where you learn it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 🧬 **Science** | DeepMind's **AlphaFold** predicts the 3D shape of proteins from their sequence. Its database holds over 200 million predicted structures, freely available to every biologist, and the work shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. | A neural network trained on known examples, then evaluated on proteins it had never seen | Weeks 3 and 4 |
| 🌦️ **Weather** | ML weather models such as Google DeepMind's **GraphCast** produce 10-day global forecasts in about a minute on a single machine, and match or beat traditional systems on many measures. | Learning patterns from decades of past data | Weeks 1 and 2 |
| 🌱 **Farming** | Phone apps such as **PlantVillage Nuru** recognise cassava diseases from a photo of a leaf, even offline, and have been used with farmers in East Africa. | Transfer learning on a small set of labelled photos | Week 5 |
| 👁️ **Health** | AI screening for diabetic eye disease helps clinics where specialist doctors are scarce, so patients can be checked during a routine visit. | A classifier where **recall** matters, because a missed case can mean blindness | Week 3 |
| 🗣️ **Language** | Communities such as **Masakhane** build translation and speech tools for African languages, so technology works in the languages people actually speak. | Language models, and data that represents the people who use them | Phase 2 |
| 💬 **Everyday tools** | Assistants like **Gemini** write, translate, summarise, explain code and answer questions in plain language. | A very large neural network trained by gradient descent on huge amounts of text | Week 4, then Phase 2 |

> [!NOTE]
> Every example on this list took years of work, and every one has limits and failure cases. Finding and stating those limits honestly is part of the job, and a big part of this track.

## 🚀 Where it is heading

Nobody can predict the future of a field that moves this fast, and you should be suspicious of anyone who says they can. These are directions that serious people are actively working on. Some will arrive sooner than expected, some later, some never.

- **A tutor for everyone, in their own language.** Patient, available at 2 a.m., able to explain the same idea ten different ways until one clicks.
- **AI as a lab partner for science.** Suggesting experiments, reading thousands of papers, and speeding up the search for new medicines and materials.
- **Smarter farming and climate adaptation.** Forecasts, early warnings and advice tuned to a single farm, not a whole region.
- **Models that run on a cheap phone, offline.** AI that does not need fast internet or an expensive laptop, so it works where most of the world actually lives.
- **Agents that do tasks, not just answer questions.** Booking, filling in forms and checking records. Powerful and risky, which is exactly why it needs careful builders.
- **Local problems solved by local builders.** The people who understand a problem best, here in Mombasa and across Africa, building the tools for it instead of waiting for someone far away to do it.

## ⚠️ Why the future needs people who understand it

Every powerful tool can also do harm, usually by accident.

- In the 2018 **Gender Shades** study, commercial face-analysis systems that looked excellent overall had error rates of up to about **35% for darker-skinned women**, compared with under **1% for lighter-skinned men**. The overall accuracy hid exactly who the systems failed.
- Language models can state false things with total confidence.
- A model trained on data from one place can quietly fail somewhere else.

Each of these is a failure of **data, evaluation or responsibility**, not of cleverness. They are the exact habits Phase 1 trains: look at the data (Week 1), compare against a baseline (Week 2), never trust a score you have not checked (Week 3), and ask in every session *who could be harmed*.

The future of this field will not be decided only by the people who can build the most powerful models. It will also be decided by the people who can tell whether a model is **actually good**, and **good for whom**.

## 🧭 Where you could fit in

You do not need to become a researcher to work in AI. A few of the paths people take:

| Path | What you would do | What you start building here |
|---|---|---|
| **ML engineer** | Build, train and ship models into real products | Phase 1 and the Phase 2 projects |
| **Data scientist** | Answer questions with data, and tell the truth about what the data says | Weeks 1 to 3 |
| **AI application developer** | Build apps on top of models like Gemini | Phase 2 |
| **Researcher** | Invent new methods and push what is possible | Week 4, then the paper club |
| **Responsible AI specialist** | Test, audit and document models so they are fair and safe | The Responsible AI moment in every session |
| **MLOps or platform engineer** | Keep models running reliably and affordably | Phase 2, together with the Cloud Track |
| **Founder** | Spot a local problem and build the company that solves it | Everything above, plus your community |

Most people end up mixing several of these. What they share is the foundation: data, models, honest evaluation and responsibility.
